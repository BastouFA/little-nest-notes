Step 1: get a pump through insurance before you buy one

Under the Affordable Care Act, most US health plans must cover breastfeeding equipment, including a breast pump you either keep or rent, for as long as you breastfeed (healthcare.gov). Grandfathered plans are the exception, and Medicaid coverage depends on your state. Your plan can decide whether it covers a manual or electric pump, and whether you get it before or after birth.

The easiest route is a medical-supply company (often called a DME supplier) that works with your insurer. It checks your coverage, gets the prescription from your provider, and ships the pump. Here's how it usually goes:

  1. Enter your due date and insurance on the supplier's site. Aeroflow Breastpumps and Babylist Health are two of the best-known.
  2. See which pumps are fully covered. Traditional pumps like the Spectra S2 Plus and Medela Pump in Style are often $0.
  3. See which pumps are an upgrade. You pay the difference between your plan's rate and the pump's price. Reported upgrade fees range roughly from $30 to $250, depending on your plan and the pump. You can usually pay with HSA or FSA money.
  4. Many plans also cover replacement parts and milk-storage bags on a schedule. Good suppliers remind you when you're eligible.

A common move: take the free traditional pump through insurance, then decide on a wearable later, once you know how you'll pump.

Do you need a wearable, a traditional pump, or both?

  • Exclusively pumping or building supply: many lactation-minded reviewers suggest a traditional double electric (like a Spectra) as the main pump, with a wearable for flexibility. Wearables are generally reported to empty the breast less fully. An IBCLC can tell you what suits your body.
  • Back at work, pumping 2–3 times a day: a mid-range or premium wearable earns its price in time saved. Battery and noise matter most.
  • Mostly nursing, pumping occasionally: a budget wearable or a $20–25 silicone collector may be all you need.

FAQ

Will my insurance cover a wearable breast pump?

Under the Affordable Care Act, most US health plans must cover a breast pump, but the plan decides which type. Standard covered pumps are usually traditional double electrics like the Spectra S2 Plus. Many wearables are available as an upgrade, where you pay the difference between your insurer's rate and the pump's price, often somewhere around $30 to $250. Grandfathered plans are exempt, and Medicaid coverage varies by state.

Can a wearable pump be your only pump?

Some people use a wearable as their only pump, but many lactation-minded reviewers suggest a traditional double electric pump as the main pump when you are building or protecting supply, with a wearable for work, the car or going out. Talk to an IBCLC lactation consultant about your own situation.

What flange size do I need?

Most pumps ship with 24 mm or similar flanges, and many people need smaller sizes. A wrong size can hurt and reduce output. Measure your nipple diameter after pumping or nursing and follow the brand's sizing guide, or ask an IBCLC. Inserts are cheap and often included with budget wearables.

Are cheap wearable pumps on Amazon any good?

Budget wearables like the Momcozy S9 Pro or MomMed S21 suit occasional pumping and tight budgets, but reviewers report weaker suction and shorter lifespans than premium models. Read recent reviews, check the return window, and treat star ratings with caution: an independent analysis of one popular pump listing in 2026 rated most of its reviews as unreliable.

How long do wearable pump batteries last?

It varies a lot. Reviews measured around 100 to 120 minutes for the Momcozy M5 and about 150 minutes for the MomMed S21, while the eufy S1 Pro's charging case gives roughly 10 sessions. Check whether a pump can run while charging: some, like the M5, cannot.
